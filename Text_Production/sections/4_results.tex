\subsubsection{Cell count}

\textcolor{red}{Could also show a microscopy picture of cell counting? -> hänge hier vmtl am besten noch eins in den Anhang, oder soll ich für alle 4 dann ein Bild in den anhang hängen?}

Cell counting was done with a phase-contrast microscope and a Neubauer improved counting chamber, only the \acrshort{woli} with an \acrfull{OD} of 0.387 was counted with a Buerker counting chamber. In the Buerker, squares were counted, in the Neubauer improved, the group squares were counted, both have a volume of 4~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{-6}~ml, as can be seen in \citet{lo-laboroptikgmbhZaehlkammer2026}. \textcolor{red}{der part hiervor vmtl eher in die methoden}


The cell-number per ml for the cultures was then determined using the following calculation:

\begin{equation}
	\textit{cellnumber} \cdot ml^{-1} = \frac{\textit{ø cellnumber}\cdot \textit{dilution factor}}{\textit{Volume of the counted Square}}
	\label{eq:cellnmbr_per_ml}
\end{equation}


\Cref{tab:cellnmr_wol_Bürk_1} through \ref{tab:cellnmr_bovi} in the appendix contain the original counted cellnumbers per filled chamber and square. To calculate the cell count per ml, the arithmetic mean was determined for each culture (tab.~\ref{tab:ø_cell_per_culture}) and substituted into the equation~\ref{eq:cellnmbr_per_ml} along with the corresponding dilution factor (see ibid.). 
The results of the calculation were recorded in a table~\ref{tab:cellnumb}. The calculation was performed as an example using \acrshort{woli}~(\acrshort{OD} = 0.387):

\begin{equation*}
	\textit{cellnumber} \cdot ml^{-1} = \frac{216.375\ \textit{cells} \cdot 20}{4 \cdot 10^{-6}\ ml} = 1,081,875,000  = 10.819 \cdot 10^{8}
	\label{eq:bsp_cellnmbr_per_ml}
\end{equation*}

A clear trend can be observed for the cellnumber per ml of the different \acrfull{mbrevi} culture throughout the table \ref{tab:cellnumb}: higher \acrshort{OD} values correspond to a higher cell count per ml. At the lowest measured \acrshort{OD} of 0.116 (\acrshort{thau}), 3.644~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{8}~cells~per~ml was determined, and at the highest \acrshort{OD} of 0.396 (\acrshort{woli}), 10.672 $\cdot$ 10\textsuperscript{8} cells per mll was determined.

Nevertheless, differences between the organisms are also apparent. For example, \acrshort{bovi} (\acrshort{OD} = 0.287) has a higher cell count per mL (9.272~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{8}) than \acrshort{milli} (\acrshort{OD} = 0.378 and celnumber~per~ml~=~8.880~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{8}) despite having a lower \acrshort{OD} of nearly 0.1. Furthermore, both \acrshort{woli} cultures and \acrshort{milli} have similar \acrshort{OD} values between 0.378 and 0.396, but those of \acrshort{woli} are around 2 $\cdot$ 10\textsuperscript{8} cells per ml higher (10.819 or 10.672 $\cdot$ 10\textsuperscript{8} to 8.880 $\cdot$ 10\textsuperscript{8} cells per ml).
 
The \acrshort{woli} cultures are quite close to each other in terms of cell count per ml, with the higher value being determined for the culture with the lower \acrshort{OD}.

Besides \acrshort{woli}, two cultures of \acrshort{thau} were also examined to determine cell count. Here, one culture has an \acrshort{OD} of 0.235 that is slightly more than twice as high as the other one with 0.116, but a cell count per ml that is slightly less than twice as high (5.603~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{8} to 3.644~$\cdot$~10\textsuperscript{8} cells per ml).

Even though the \acrshort{OD} of the cultures used was not measured prior to plating, the plating efficiency was determined. This was done on the assumption that the cultures that appeared visually full-grown had an \acrshort{OD} approximately equal to those listed in tab. \ref{tab:cellnumb}, with the higher value for cell count per mL always being used. 

\begin{equation}
	\textit{Plating efficiency [ppm]} = \frac{\textit{counted CFU}}{\textit{number of used cells}} \cdot\ 10^6
	\label{eq:plat_eff}
\end{equation}

The calculation was based on equation \ref{eq:plat_eff} and was performed as follows, for example:

\begin{equation*}
	\textit{Plating efficiency} = \frac{2432}{0.2\ ml \cdot 10.819 \cdot 10^8\ cells \cdot ml^{-1}} \cdot 10^6 = 11.24\ ppm	
\end{equation*}

Until the final measurement day (day 10), the OD decreases continuously to an OD600 value of around 0.1. One difference that can still be noted here, however, is that the variation between the triplicates at 0~µg/ml is smaller than that observed with \acrshort{puro}. At 20 µg/ml, one replicate shows no growth at all by the end of the measurement (Cltr.~2). The other two replicates do not begin exponential growth until day 8 or 9. The maximum is reached on day 10 at approximately 0.1. By day 11, a slight decrease in the OD600 values can already be observed.\\

\subsubsection{Nucleotide-baseanalogica sensitivity test}

\textbf{\acrlong{azaeight}}

Similar to the \acrshort{ab} sensitivity test, a growth experiment was conducted for the base analogs \acrfull{azaeight} for \acrshort{thau}, \acrshort{bovi}, \acrshort{milli}, and \acrfull{azasix} for \acrshort{woli}. One difference is that the base analogs do not dissolve in \ch{H2O}, so they were dissolved in \acrfull{dmso}. Furthermore, different concentrations were selected: 0~µg/ml, 100~µg/ml, 250~µg/ml, 500~µg/ml, 750~µg/ml, 1000~µg/ml, and one with 1~ml~\acrshort{dmso}.
The \acrshort{OD} was measured and plotted on a logarithmic scale against time (see Figures \ref{fig:aza_mbov} through \ref{fig:aza_mwoli}). The original measured data is in the appendix (Fig. \textcolor{red}{X}). \\

Thus, exponential growth occurs within the first two days. On days 3 through 4, the \acrshort{OD} reaches a maximum of around 0.3, followed by a continuous decline with a final \acrshort{OD} of just under 0.1 on day 13.\\

The solvent control (DSMO), in which no base analogs were added but only 1~ml of the solvent \acrshort{dmso}, showed a different growth curve. Initially, none of the triplicates begin to grow immediately; instead, the \acrshort{OD} values first decrease. On day 3, slight growth begins, reaching a maximum of 0.1 on days 4–6. The three remaining \acrshort{azaeight} concentration solutions (500, 750, and 1000~µg/ml) exhibit a very similar growth curve. An \acrshort{OD} below 0.01 was measured for \acrshort{bovi} during the first 4 to 6 days. Then there was an increase to an \acrshort{OD} of 0.03, which remained constant until the final measurement day (day 13). An exception is Culture 2 in the 1000~µg/ml \acrshort{azaeight} culture. This culture grows from Day 3 to Day 5 to an \acrshort{OD} of 0.1, which then decreases slightly until the end of the measurement series.\\

\subsection{Geography}

All methane emission analyses focused on three countries: \acrfull{deu}, \acrfull{nzl} and \acrfull{usa}. In addition, only the anthropogenic emission will be investigated.

\subsubsection{Total methane emissions of a country}

\Cref{fig:sectors_emission} shows the development of the total methane emission of a year as well as of the specific sectors from 1990 to 2020s for each country. 

In general, the first thing that stands out is that the time series for the U.S. ends as early as 2022, while those for the other two countries do not end until 2024. Furthermore, the scale of methane emissions shows that the U.S. generally produces significantly more \ch{CH4}; the maximum total emissions are 35,000 kt \ch{CH4}, while for \acrshort{deu} the maximum is 5 Tg and for \acrshort{nzl} it is 1.5 Tg.\\


\begin{figure}[!h]	
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{figures/Results/GEO/All_CC_all_sectors.pdf}
	\caption{Time series of the anthropogenic methane emission of \acrfull{deu}, \acrfull{nzl} and \acrfull{usa}. The different relevant emission sectors are shown with their total sum (Total). Furthermore, the total emission minus the \ch{CH4} produced in \acrfull{enterfe} processes of cattle is plotted as well. Methane is plotted as Tg \ch{CH4} of a year against the time.}
	\label{fig:sectors_emission}
\end{figure}

For \acrfull{deu}, a clear downward trend is evident in total \ch{CH4} emissions (see \cref{fig:sectors_emission}). Emissions fell from 3 Tg \ch{CH4} in 1990 (5 Tg) to 2 Tg in 2024, representing a decrease of approximately 60\%. Two distinct phases of this trend can be identified: first, from 1990 to 2006, with a sharp decline of about 2.5~Tg \ch{CH4} (approximately 2.6 Tg \ch{CH4} in 2006), followed by a phase with a more moderate decline to 2 Tg of anthropogenically produced methane in 2024.

Total emissions excluding those from \acrfull{enterfe} in cattle (total excluding cattle \acrshort{enterfe}) follow a similar trend, with the curve’s value being only 1~Tg~\ch{CH4} lower.\\

Looking at the sectoral emissions of \acrshort{deu}, it becomes apparent that in 1990 there were three equally large \ch{CH4}-producing sectors: “Energy,” “Waste,” and “Agriculture,” each accounting for approximately 1.5 Tg \ch{CH4} (see \cref{fig:sectors_emission}). However, these sectors have developed differently.

The energy and waste sectors have followed quite similar trends. Following a sharp decline between 1990 and 2006—with the waste sector not beginning to change until 1994—from 1.5 to 0.5~Tg, a moderate decline then sets in, until approximately 0.025 Tg of \ch{CH4} is emitted in 2024.

Agriculture is also changing, though only very slightly compared to the other two sectors: from 1.6 Tg of methane in 1990 to approximately 1.25 Tg \ch{CH4} in 2024, with the largest single change occurring between 1990 and 1991, amounting to a difference of about 0.1~Tg. In 2015, total emissions excluding cattle \acrshort{enterfe} are exactly as high as those of the agricultural sector and fall below that level thereafter.

In addition to these three sectors, two others are shown in \cref{fig:sectors_emission}.

The “Industrial Processes \& Product Use” sector is a negligibly small source of methane emissions. Its \ch{CH4} emissions have remained consistently well below 0.1 Tg over the 35-year period.

With \ch{CH4} emissions consistently ranging between 0.3 and 0.4 Tg, the \acrfull{lulucf} sector was the fourth-largest single methane emission sector in \acrlong{deu} during the 1990s,
but it surpassed the waste sector in 2016 and has been the second-largest \ch{CH4}-emitting sector in \acrshort{deu} after agriculture since 2019.\\

For the \acrshort{usa} the difference between total methane emissions and total emissions excluding cattle \acrshort{enterfe} remains fairly constant at around 10 to 15 Tg \ch{CH4} (see \cref{fig:sectors_emission}).
In addition, a clear, steady decline can be observed for the total methane emission from 1990 (approx. 33 Tg \ch{CH4}) to 2022 (approx. 27 Tg \ch{CH4}), although the curve does not follow the trend linearly but rather fluctuates slightly. Over this 32-year period, the U.S. has reduced its methane emissions across all sectors by about 6~Tg~\ch{CH4}, which corresponds to a reduction of 18\%.\\

As in \acrlong{deu}, the three most significant methane-producing sectors in the U.S. are “Energy,” “Agriculture,” and “Waste” in 1990; however, here the energy sector is the largest contributor, accounting for nearly half of the emissions in 1990 with 15 Tg \ch{CH4}, followed by the waste and agriculture sectors with approximately 8 Tg \ch{CH4} each.

The energy and waste sectors show a downward trend over the time series (energy: from 15 to 10 Tg \ch{CH4} and waste: from 8 to 5 Tg \ch{CH4}), while emissions from agriculture rise slightly over time, from 8.5 Tg to 10~Tg \ch{CH4}. Thus, in 2022, agriculture and energy—each at 10 Tg \ch{CH4}—are the largest methane emission sectors and together account for more than two-thirds of total methane emissions in the U.S. in 2022.

Two other sectors contribute to U.S. methane emissions: the “Industrial Processes \& Product Use” sector, which is relatively small and therefore negligible, and the “LULUCF” sector, with a constant range of 0.2 to 0.25 Tg \ch{CH4}.\\

Compared to the other two countries, \acrlong{nzl} presents a different picture (see \cref{fig:sectors_emission}).

First, total \ch{CH4}emissions in 2024 are nearly the same as those in 1990, namely about 1.4~Tg \ch{CH4}; furthermore, a slight increase in emissions to 1.5~Tg~\ch{CH4} can be observed through 2006, followed by a slight decline.

Second, “agriculture” is the dominant sector in terms of methane emissions and accounts for nearly 90\% of \ch{CH4} emissions from \acrshort{nzl} (see \cref{fig:sectors_emission}). All other sectors account for significantly less than 0.1~Tg~\ch{CH4} per year, with the exception of the waste sector, whose methane emissions are slightly above 0.1 Tg~\ch{CH4}.

Third, the difference between total methane emissions and total emissions excluding cattle \acrshort{enterfe} increases by about 60\% (0.5 Tg \ch{CH4} in 1990 and 0.8 Tg in 2024).

\subsubsection{Enteric fermentation processes of cattle}

\textbf{Methane emissions}

\Cref{fig:enterFe_emission_CH4} shows the trend in methane emissions from enteric fermentation in cattle, as well as their subcategories “dairy cattle” and “non-dairy cattle” for each country. The values for the subcategories are calculated according to \cref{eq:emission_total} and based on the required data from the reported \acrshort{crt} (visualized in \cref{fig:indic_deu} through \ref{fig:indic_usa}). Total emissions from cattle are therefore simply the sum of these values.\\

Cattle emissions for \acrfull{deu} in \cref{fig:enterFe_emission_CH4} decline from 1990 to 2024 (from 1.25~Tg~\ch{CH4} to approximately 0.8 Tg), with a steeper decline in the first year of the time series, followed by a more gradual decline.

For dairy and non-dairy cattle, this initial decline is also evident in \cref{fig:enterFe_emission_CH4}, after which a slight decline can also be observed (dairy cattle: 0.75~Tg~\ch{CH4} in 1990 to 0.5 Tg in 2024; non-dairy cattle: 0.55 in 1990 to 0.35 in 2024). Dairy cattle are an exception: their emissions drop to 0.5 Tg \ch{CH4} by 2006, then rise slightly again through 2015, and subsequently decline once more, returning to 0.5 Tg by 2014.\\

Dairy cattle consistently produce slightly more \ch{CH4} per year than non-dairy cattle (0.1–0.2~Tg \ch{CH4}, see \cref{fig:enterFe_emission_CH4}. This contrasts with the herd ratio shown in \cref{fig:indic_deu}, where the dairy cattle herd generally accounts for about half of the non-dairy cattle herd over the years.

Further differences in the indicators are evident for Ym, where dairy cattle had a significantly higher value of 7\% in 1990 compared to 6.25, which, however, declines sharply to 6.1\% by 2024, while the value for non-dairy cattle rises to 6.5\%, as well as for GE, where the value for the non-dairy cattle herd remains constant at 100~MJ~head\textsuperscript{-1}~day\textsuperscript{-1}, while the value for the dairy cattle herd rises from 250 to 380~MJ~head\textsuperscript{-1}~day\ \textsuperscript{-1}.\\

Methane emissions from cattle in \acrshort{enterfe} for \acrlong{nzl} rise from 0.5 to 0.75~Tg~\ch{CH4}, while the values have remained relatively constant since 2014.

An increase is also evident for dairy cattle, which has plateaued since 2014 (0.25~Tg~\ch{CH4} in 1990 to 0.55~Tg in 2024). Non-dairy cattle, on the other hand, show similar values of around 0.5~Tg~\ch{CH4} in both 1990 and 2024.

Thus, in 1990, dairy and non-dairy cattle contributed equally to methane emissions from the cattle population; however, this share has changed over the years for dairy cows, so that by 2024 they will account for about two-thirds of the emissions. This shift in the ratio is quite similar across the subcategory populations (see \cref{fig:indic_nzl}). Furthermore, the energy efficiency (GE) values for non-dairy cattle remain constant at 150~MJ~head\textsuperscript{-1}~day\textsuperscript{-1}, while the value for dairy cattle rises from 160 to 225~MJ~head\textsuperscript{-1}~day\textsuperscript{-1}.\\

In the \acrshort{usa}, methane emissions from dairy cattle rise from just under 2~Tg~\ch{CH4} to 2~Tg, with the slight increase not beginning until around 2005. The other two livestock categories, on the other hand, fluctuate overall—for both non-dairy and dairy herds—between 4.9 and 5.5~Tg for non-dairy livestock and between 6.5~Tg~\ch{CH4} and 7.1~Tg for dairy livestock (see \cref{fig:enterFe_emission_CH4}). Unlike in the other two countries, non-dairy cattle here emit significantly more than dairy cattle (2024: 5~Tg~\ch{CH4} versus 2~Tg).

As with the other countries, the emission curves correspond fairly well with the livestock numbers from \cref{fig:indic_usa}, where non-dairy cattle—at 80 million head—are kept in significantly greater numbers than dairy cattle (20 million head).
The curve for dairy cattle does not match the livestock numbers exactly, as these remain constant. However, the GE for dairy cattle rises steadily from 200 to 260 MJ/animal·day between 1990 and 2022, while the Ym value decreases from 6.5\% to 6.1\% over the same period.

\subsubsection{Prediction for 2030}

\Cref{fig:pred_red_sze_deu} (\acrshort{deu}), \ref{fig:pred_red_sze_nzl} (\acrshort{nzl}) and \ref{fig:pred_red_sze_usa} (\acrshort{usa}) show the total methane emission for each country as a time series from 1990 to the last published year in the CRT and beyond until 2030 as a forecast. The forecast is shown for three different linear models (using 5, 10, or 15 years as the reference period for the fit) and for a scenario in which no further intervention was made, as well as for three scenarios with varying degrees of success in cattle immunization (10, 30, and 50\% shifts in the rumen microbiome). The equations as well as the R\textsuperscript{2} and \acrfull{ci} values are given in the annex in \cref{}\\

A similar trend is in all three figures visible for the three linear fits used. All models show a negative developement, although the max. \acrfull{ci} of the 5 year model has a positive trend. 

Besides, the forecast for the fit with 10 or 15 years as time reference show very similar prediction while the 5-year linear model differs from them. But in \acrshort{deu} the 5-year fit predicts higher values for \ch{CH4} production for 2030 than the other two models, while for \acrshort{nzl} and \acrshort{usa} the fits with the longer time sample as reference higher values predicts. 

Two additional points stand out when comparing Germany with the other two countries (\cref{fig:pred_red_sze_deu} to \cref{fig:pred_red_sze_nzl} and \ref{fig:pred_red_sze_usa}). 

First, in Germany, the predicted range of emission values at least partially meets the two emission targets set by the \acrfull{gmp}, whereas in the other two countries, the emission values exceed the targets.

Second, the value for the 60\% of 2010 emissions target is higher than the 70\% of 2020 emissions target for \acrshort{deu}. For the other two countries, it is the other way around. Even so, the two values are in a similar range for all of them.\\

A closer look at the values in \cref{fig:pred_red_sze_deu} for Germany shows that the five-year model for predicting the emissions trend, assuming unchanged mitigation efforts, forecasts a nearly constant trend with a slight decline (from 1.9~Tg~\ch{CH4} to 1.8~Tg), although the confidence intervals here are quite wide (see \cref{label} from \textcolor{red}{X to Y}), and an increase in the values is even possible here.

The 10- and 15-year models, on the other hand, show a clear downward trend with a value of approximately 1.5~Tg~\ch{CH4} in 2030, which corresponds almost exactly to the 60\% target of the \acrshort{gmp}. The range defined by the \acrshort{ci} is also significantly narrower here, ranging from about 1.4 to 1.75~Tg, with the 70\%-target remaining just above the lower \acrshort{ci} range.

The cumulative range of all three \acrlong{ci} values points to a range of approximately 1.4~Tg to 2.25~Tg~\ch{CH4}. \\

The projected values for the reduction scenario 10 hardly differs from the scenario without mitigation measures and deviates by only about 0.1~Tg (see \cref{fig:pred_red_sze_deu}).

A greater difference can be observed for Scenario 30. Here, the projected emissions for the 5-year adjustment would be roughly at the level of the \acrshort{gmp} target of 60\% of 2010 emissions and thus at values comparable to those in the 10- and 15-year models under “Projection without vaccination.” For this scenario, the values for these two models under “with immunization” are just below the target of 70\% of 2020 emissions, namely at approximately 1.3~Tg~\ch{CH4}. The range of cumulative \acrshort{ci} for the year 2030 extends from 1.1~Tg to just under 2~Tg of emitted \ch{CH4}. This means, for these models assuming a 30\% change in the rumen microbiome, no further increase in methane levels would be expected until 2030.\\

The scenario 50 with an immunisation of cattle that leads to a 50\% shift of microbiome towards less methan production, had an combined \acrshort{ci} range from slightly below 1~Tg to 1.75~Tg~\ch{CH4} emitted in 2030. Where the models and their \acrshort{ci}s would be completly below both \acrshort{gmp} emission targets for 2030 (see \cref{fig:pred_red_sze_deu}, approx. slightly below 1~Tg to 1.3~Tg~\ch{CH4}).

In addition, the prediction for the 5-year linear fit would be 2030 at nearly exact the 70\% emissions of 2020 (around 1.4~Tg~\ch{CH4}), although the max. \acrshort{ci} value is with 1.75~Tg clearly above both emission aims.\\

For the U.S., the 10- and 15-year models for "prediction without immunization" in \cref{fig:pred_red_sze_usa} predict an emissions value for 2030 that corresponds almost exactly to the 2022 value (approximately 27~Tg~\ch{CH4}).
