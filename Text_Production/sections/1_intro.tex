The pressing situation of the planets "health" is a scientific consensus \citep{ipccClimateChange20222022, planetaryboundariessciencepbsciencePlanetaryHealthCheck2025}. We can already see the negative impact in our day to day live that the taken projecteries over the last decades are to be proven true, so a temperature rise in Europe of 2.5\,°C towards preindustrial times can be measured as well as a higher frequency of \gls{compound} \citep{ionitaDroughtsHotterDroughts2026, c3s/ecmwfC3SWMOEuropeanState2026}. This led to an extreme heat wave this summer (2026) in Europe, which, in Germany alone, is estimated to have resulted in more than 14\,000 deaths \parencite{rkiWochenberichtZurHitzebedingten2026}.Therefore, it is highly necessary to develop strategies for adaption to this changing environment, but more so for mitigation to stabilize the climate and to keep at least the 2\,°C goal of the Paris Agreement \parencite{unfcccParisAgreement2015}. 

One of the main driver for this development is the anthropogenic climate change. Climate change is one of nine processes that are relevant for containing a stable system on earth, to grant condition, in which human mankind can knowingly thrive \parencite{planetaryboundariessciencepbsciencePlanetaryHealthCheck2025}. Like six other of them, climate change exceeded its \gls{planetaryBoundarysGLS} (\acrshort{pb}) already (ibid.).

The relevant value for climate change is the anthropogenic \gls{radForce}, which not only exceeded the \acrshort{pb} (+1~W/m\textsuperscript{2}) 2025 but the boundary for the \gls{zoneOfIncreasingRisk} (+1.5~W/m\textsuperscript{2}) as well with a value of 2.97~W/m\textsuperscript{2} \parencite[p.\,13]{planetaryboundariessciencepbsciencePlanetaryHealthCheck2025, irfanChapterEightRadiative2024}. \Cref{fig:GHG_respons_temp_change} shows the impact of this on global temperature since the Industrial Revolution, with an increase of just under 1.7\,°C.
It also shows how much of this increase is caused by each of the three most important anthropogenic \acrfull{ghg} -\ch{CO2}, \ch{CH4}, and \ch{N2O} - broken down by emission source (“Fossil Fuels and Industry” or “Agriculture and Land”). Nitrous dioxide has a minor impact and is responsible for a total of approximately 0.1 \,°C. \ch{CO2}, on the other hand, has the biggest responsibility for the temperature rise (more than 50\%, \cref{fig:GHG_respons_temp_change}) due to his high abundance in the atmosphere (423~ppm in 2025) \parencite{teamAnnualGreenhouseGas2025, planetaryboundariessciencepbsciencePlanetaryHealthCheck2025}.\\

The second most abundant \acrshort{ghg} and with the biggest relative increase in atmospheric concentration (around 265\% from 720~ppb 1750 to 1935~ppb in 2026) is methane \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025, planetaryboundariessciencepbsciencePlanetaryHealthCheck2025, thoningTrendsGloballyaveragedCH42022}. Methane is also responsible (\cref{fig:GHG_respons_temp_change}) for around 30\% (\gls{gross}) of the anthropogenic temperature rise \citep[p.\,9]{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025}. If compared to \ch{CO2} some features makes it highly interesting as a potential target for mitigation. 
The stronger impact of methane on radiative forcing and its relatively short lifetime in the atmosphere of 12.4~years (15-40\% of \ch{CO2} is still in the atmosphere after 1000 years) \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025} lead to \acrlong{gwp} (\gls{gwpGls}) values that strongly decline over longer time horizons (\gls{gwpGls}20: 79.7 and \gls{gwpGls}100: 27 for non-fossil \ch{CH4} in \textcite[Table~7.15]{IPPCC_AR6_Chapter7_GWP}).

This means that, as a mitigation strategy addressing \ch{CH4}, reducing methane emissions is sufficient to achieve a notable impact within two decades, whereas mitigation strategies for carbon dioxide must also focus on the removal of \ch{CO2} from the atmosphere. Because of that, international initiatives aim at reducing methane emissions such as the \acrfull{gmp}, which was initiated in 2021 and signed by 160 states including \acrfull{deu}, \acrfull{nzl} and \acrfull{usa} \parencite{ccacHomepageGlobalMethane}.The participants commit to emit 30\% less methane than they did in 2020 and 40\% less than in 2010, without any penalties \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025}.

However, right now methane is still accumulating in the atmosphere \parencite{saunoisGlobalMethaneBudget2025, thoningTrendsGloballyaveragedCH42022}. Globally the three biggest sources for anthropogenic \ch{CH4} are the agriculture- (approx 40\%), energy- (36\%) and waste-sector (22\%) \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025, mundraEmergentMethaneMitigation2024}. The emission derived from the energy and waste sector can be reduced with today's technical knowledge and options quit easily \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025, intergovernmentalpanelonclimatechangeipccAgricultureForestryOther2022}. Emissions from agriculture, on the other hand, are counted as "hard-to-abate", because the world is lacking technologies and strategies that are socially and political accepted \parencite{ edelenboschReducingSectoralHardtoabate2024, thoningTrendsGloballyaveragedCH42022, intergovernmentalpanelonclimatechangeipccAgricultureForestryOther2022}.
This is due to the fact that methane arises naturally in agriculture from biological processes, primarily through enteric fermentation, especially in cattle (77\%), and manure management. Together they make up for 31\% of global methane emission \parencite{appelhansUnterschatztesTreibhausgasMethan2025, smithAgriculturalMethaneEmissions2021}.
The only option for reducing methane emissions for this right now is to reduce the number of live stock dramatically, which is contrary to the global rise of demand for livestock products and globally rising numbers of livestock \parencite{faoLivestockBalance2009, edelenboschReducingSectoralHardtoabate2024}. \\
This bachelor thesis wants to attack this lack of options by further investigating a mitigation strategy for the largest single source of methane emissions in the agricultural sector: "immunization of cattle" against its methanogenic microbiome in the rumen. For this purpose, an interdisciplinary approach was chosen with a biochemical and geographical part, which allows uniting two scales of investigation in one work.
 
The biochemistry site investigates new approaches to attack methane production on cellular or organism level, while the geographical perspective places the results in the context of multifactorial climate change and assess the extent to which the biochemical approach can play a role to mitigate.

\subsection{Methane production in cattle farming and a possible mitigation}
\label{sec:intro_biochem}

Cattle as a ruminant has a rumen which enables them to break down and digest long fibers of carbohydrates such as plant cells via microbial fermentation \parencite{mossMethaneProductionRuminants2000, epaInventoryUSGreenhouse2024}. These fermentation processes carried out by a wide variety of microbes produce \ch{H2} and \ch{CO2}, which must be removed as quickly as possible due to the negative feedback effect on fermentation. This is done in the rumen by strictly anaerobic microbes, which utilize the \ch{H2} and \ch{CO2} as substrates and in the process produce a variety of substances \parencite{mossMethaneProductionRuminants2000}.  Two relevant groups are acetogenic bacteria, which produce short-chain hydrocarbons, or  methanogenic archaea, which produce \ch{CH4} \parencite{boadiMitigationStrategiesReduce2004, garnettGreenhousegasAbatementAustralian2024}. 
In the rumen, methanogenic archaea are often the dominant group, as they can metabolize \ch{H2} and \ch{CO2} more efficient \parencite{mossMethaneProductionRuminants2000}. This also means that completely eliminating methanogenic \gls{archa} would not be advisable since, according to \textcite{morvanEstablishmentHydrogenutilizingBacteria1994}, \ch{H2} would accumulate, making digestion in cattle less efficient.

However, the goal of the immunization strategy is to shift the microbiome toward a higher proportion of acetogenic bacteria by training the cattle’s immune system to recognize and attack methanogenic archaea \parencite{malyuginaMitigationStrategiesMethane2025}. This would result, on the one hand, in less methane production and, on the other hand, in the production of more short-chain hydrocarbons as acetate or butyrate that can still be digested and absorbed by the cattle. In theory, this would also lead to a more efficient digestion \parencite{boadiMitigationStrategiesReduce2004, mossMethaneProductionRuminants2000, malyuginaMitigationStrategiesMethane2025}.

Already, there were some experiments trying to immunize cattle against methanogenic \gls{archa} with an inactivated vaccine. But the success was low and inconsistent, mainly due to high surface variability of methanogens  \parencite{wrightReducingMethaneEmissions2004, malyuginaMitigationStrategiesMethane2025}.To solve this problem a robust and specific antigen has to be found for the dominant methanogen species. 
The problem here, however, is that while the composition of anaerobic, methanogenic archaea in the rumen is now somewhat better understood (four main groups and the dominant genus is \acrfull{mbrevi}, which accounts for approximately 60\%), the individual species remain poorly understood and characterized, even though some of them have already been isolated \citep{baca-gonzalezAreVaccinesSolution2020}.\\

Here, the strains \acrfull{thau} and \acrfull{bovi} isolated from the rumen of cattle will be further investigated \parencite{millerDescriptionMethanobrevibacterGottschalkii2002, leeMethanobrevibacterBoviskoreaniSp2013}. For this purpose, the investigation will have two sites.
First, investigate growth behavior of the cell under various conditions.
Second, trying to implement for the first time a genetic tools for \acrfull{mbrevi}. Following the approach of \textcite{kleinMarkerlessMutagenesisEnables2025} for markerless mutagenesis of \textit{Methanothermobacter marburgensis}, \acrlong{mbrevi} is transformed via interdomain \gls{conjug} with \acrfull{ecol}.
Therby a \gls{suicide} was used, with four main functional sites to allow the selection of transformed \acrfull{mbrevi} and also the counterselection to enable genome editing (\cref{fig:general_plasmid_map})

The first functional site is the "\acrshort{ecol} cassette + conjugation-gen". This is important for preparing the donor organism (\acrshort{ecol}) for the conjugation to transform the \acrlong{mbrevi} species. Therefor the plasmid contains for the \gls{trafoglo} of \acrshort{ecol} an \gls{ori} and a selection marker (\textit{catP}) against chloramphenicol. In addition, the \textit{traJ}-gene is needed for the conjugation.
The remaining sites on the plasmid are for the \acrlong{mbrevi} species. So the \textit{pac}-gen with its promoter is the selection marker for transformed \acrshort{mbrevi} against \acrfull{puro}. The "Homology arms" site contains the segments of the \acrfull{gDna} from the transforming \acrshort{mbrevi}. The homology regions thereby flank the gene to be deleted. Through single homologous recombination events, the plasmid is first integrated and then removed again via counterselection, whereby the gene flanked by the homology arms is also excised \parencite{kleinMarkerlessMutagenesisEnables2025}. The last of the four sites is \acrfull{hpt} gene, which enhances sensitivity against base-analogue \acrfull{azaeight} for the counterselection.

In the following experiments, regions on the homology arms are selected that flank the \textit{\acrshort{hpt}} of the respective organism, resulting in a mutant lacking genomic \textit{\acrshort{hpt}}. This makes it possible to target any other gene in the genome by selecting the appropriate homology regions and removing them via markerless counterselection \parencite{kleinMarkerlessMutagenesisEnables2025}.


\subsection{Evaluation based on calculated \ch{CH4} emission}
\label{sec:intro_geo}

Based on a closer examination of three countries (\acrshort{deu}, \acrshort{nzl}, \acrshort{usa}), the mitigation strategy “immunization of cattle” will be evaluated. First, it has to be checked if the global methane emission patterns earlier in the introduction fit on the national scale and cattle is a valid target for mitigation.
Second, the calculation of methane emission is based on the guideline developed by the \acrfull{ipcc} in the year 2006 \parencite{IPCC_2006_complete_Guidline} and its refinement in 2019 \parencite{IPCC_2019_Chapter_EnterFE}. This guideline standardized the international calculation of national \acrshort{ghg} emission for all emission sources.

To calculate methane emissions resulting from enteric fermentation processes in cattle \parencite{ipccChapter10Emission2006, IPCC_2019_Chapter_EnterFE}, cattle are typically divided into two subcategories: “dairy” and “non-dairy” cattle. Emissions are first calculated separately for these two categories and then summed. There are three different calculation methods (Tier 1 through 3), which differ in their complexity and the amount of data required.

The Tier 1 approach is quite straightforward. The number of animals in the corresponding animal category is multiplied by the appropriate \acrfull{ef}, which is provided as a default value by the \acrshort{ipcc} and specifies how much methane is emitted per animal \parencite[Eq.\,10.19]{ipccChapter10Emission2006}.

In Tier 2, this \acrshort{ef} is now calculated for each animal category and country based on specific local activity data. Here, the \acrfull{GE} per animal is calculated from energy consumption and the average \acrfull{DE}. The \acrshort{ef} is then obtained by multiplying the \acrshort{GE} by the \acrfull{Ym}, which can be taken as the default value from the \acrshort{ipcc} or has been scientifically determined for the respective country and animal category \parencite[Eq.\,10.21]{ipccChapter10Emission2006}.

Tier 3 involves even more complex calculations and uses detailed, mechanistic models or direct measurements at the farm or animal level (e.g., individual feeding data, respiratory gas measurements of individual animals) \parencite{IPCC_2006_complete_Guidline}.\\


The mitigation strategy "immunization of cattle" influences clearly impact two values of the Tier 2 approach. First the \acrshort{Ym}, whereas a shift in the rumen microbiome towards non-methanogenic microbes leads logically to a lower energy conversion into methane. Second the \acrshort{DE}, as described in \cref{sec:intro_biochem}, the microbiome shift is expected to lead to increased digestive efficiency, since less energy is converted into methane, which cannot be utilized, and more into short-chain hydrocarbons that can be absorbed by the cattle.
The performed calculation of methane emission followed the Tier 2 approach and this thought as can be seen further in \cref{sec:data_and_material_geo} and \cref{sec:methane_emis_calc_method}.
